R21 · Evaluation and measurement

ICC must distinguish absolute agreement from similar score rankings

Intraclass correlation – A discussion and demonstration of basic features

David Liljequist, Britt Elfving, Kirsti Skavberg Roaldsen · 2019
PLOS ONE 14(7):e0219854

Publisher / author sourceOriginal local PDF (35 pages)Cited passages and highlightsBack to references

What the study investigates

Different forms of intraclass correlation yield different interpretations of the same data. The article explains how variation among subjects, assessor bias, and error affect the estimates.

How it was studied

The authors combine theoretical derivations, Monte Carlo simulation, and clinical examples to compare ICC(1), ICC(A,1), and ICC(C,1), including absolute agreement, consistency, and confidence intervals.

Findings

An assessor who systematically adds several points can preserve score ordering while reducing absolute agreement. Coefficients also depend on sample heterogeneity and bias structure, and should be interpreted with intervals and model assumptions.

Limits of the evidence

ICC requires appropriate continuous or approximately continuous measures. It should not be forced onto binary decisions or a few ordinal labels. A high ICC does not rule out occasional critical safety errors.

How it informs DualGraph-OSCE

Unless CUHK approves a numeric score conversion, the main analysis uses item categories and exact agreement. If a defensible numeric scale is approved, absolute-agreement ICC(A,1), mean absolute error, and systematic bias can be secondary analyses. The same student's multiple stations must not be counted as independent students.

Where to cite it

Evaluation: optional continuous-score analysis and why a correlation alone is insufficient.

Possible manuscript wording

Continuous scores are compared through absolute agreement, confidence intervals, and bias rather than ranking alone. Categorical decisions are reported with confusion matrices and suitable agreement measures. [R21·1]
